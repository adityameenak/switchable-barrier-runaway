\begin{abstract}
\noindent A thermal barrier between lithium-ion cells faces a conflict. A conductive
barrier spreads heat to the cooling system in normal operation but carries a runaway
cell's heat to its neighbours. An insulating barrier blocks propagation but traps heat
during normal use. A barrier whose conductivity drops sharply above a switch
temperature could, in principle, do both. This report quantifies that tradeoff with a
one-dimensional transient finite-volume model of a five-cell stack, using single-step
Arrhenius self-heating, a smooth reversible switch law $k(T)$ and convective cooling at
the stack ends. With illustrative parameters, a 2\,mm switchable barrier holds the
normal-operation peak temperature at the conductive barrier's 38.0\,$^\circ$C (against
64.5\,$^\circ$C for an aerogel-like insulator) while confining runaway to the trigger
cell, whereas a conductive barrier lets runaway spread to all five cells within about a
minute. A sweep of 361 barrier designs shows that containment is governed by a single
quantity, the hot-state barrier conductance $\koff/L$, which must stay below about
32\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$ for the chosen end cooling. A switch-temperature sweep finds a
window of about 38--175\,$^\circ$C in which the switch both cools and protects. The
solver conserves energy to round-off and is verified against analytical, ODE-solver and
convergence tests. The convergence study also showed that onset-fitted Arrhenius
kinetics, extrapolated to burning-cell temperatures, produce a reaction front too thin
for any practical mesh to resolve; a rate ceiling restores mesh convergence. The model
is then extended with tabulated $k(T)$, latent heat and a delayed, one-way switch, and six
real barrier materials are compared using datasheet and literature properties. Static
insulators either contain runaway only with a large normal-use penalty (silica aerogel,
79\,$^\circ$C at its 2.25\,mm containment thickness; ceramic fibre, 66\,$^\circ$C at
4.25\,mm) or need thick layers (mica, 6.25\,mm), and a conductive gap pad and a
paraffin/graphite phase-change composite do not contain it at any thickness up to
10\,mm. A published graphene/microsphere thermal switch contains runaway at 3.5\,mm while
keeping the normal-use peak within 1\,K of the ideal conductor, and is the best of the six
under every sensitivity case tested. Cell and kinetic parameters remain order-of-magnitude
placeholders, so the conclusions are qualitative. Finally, 452 filled-silicone formulations
(particles, silver nanowires, nanoparticle--nanowire hybrids, endothermic fillers and
expandable microspheres) are screened with an effective-medium model extended for
fin-limited nanowires, percolating wire networks and microsphere expansion, and
checked against measured nanowire composites. Static formulations need at least
5.6\,mm to contain runaway, and 8.5\,mm or more to keep the normal-use peak near
44\,$^\circ$C. The recommended
formulation is silicone with 40\,vol\% expandable microspheres and 20\,vol\% Al(OH)$_3$,
which contains runaway at 2.9\,mm (3.5\,mm in the worst case tested) at a 42\,$^\circ$C
normal-use peak. Conductive nanowire networks raise cold-state conductivity but are
counterproductive unless the expansion breaks them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Thermal runaway in a lithium-ion cell is a self-accelerating sequence of exothermic
decomposition reactions that can drive the cell to several hundred degrees and lead to
fire~\cite{feng2018review,wang2012fire,bandhauer2011}. In a module, the heat released by
one failing cell can trigger runaway in its neighbours, as multi-cell abuse experiments
have shown repeatedly~\cite{lamb2015,lopez2015propagation}. Preventing this cell-to-cell
propagation is a central goal of battery-pack safety design. The usual approach places a
thermal barrier between cells, which creates a design conflict:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{conductive} barrier helps move the heat cells generate in normal
        operation toward the cooling system, but also passes a runaway cell's heat
        directly to its neighbour;
  \item an \emph{insulating} barrier, for example an aerogel sheet, blocks propagation
        but raises cell temperatures in everyday use, which accelerates ageing.
\end{itemize}
Temperature-responsive barriers and thermal switches, whose conductivity changes with
temperature, are an active research topic as a way out of this
conflict~\cite{wang2024regulator,li2025switches}.

\paragraph{Related work.}
Thermal abuse of single cells is commonly modelled by coupling heat conduction to
Arrhenius kinetics for a set of decomposition reactions, an approach established for
cylindrical and prismatic cells~\cite{hatchard2001}, applied to abuse behaviour of
high-power cells~\cite{spotnitz2003}, extended to three dimensions~\cite{kim2007abuse}
and combined with experiments~\cite{lopez2015abuse}. Propagation models extend this to
packs and modules, with lumped~\cite{feng2015propagation} and three-dimensional
descriptions~\cite{feng2016module} and analyses of the heat-transfer paths between
cells~\cite{tang2020propagation}. On the mitigation side, studies have examined
phase-change composites~\cite{wilke2017pcm}, aerogel-based
insulation~\cite{wong2024aerogel}, model-based optimisation of heat-absorbing
interstitial barriers~\cite{menz2023barriers} and polymer barrier
materials~\cite{li2026polymer}. These barriers are static: their conductivity does not
respond to the operating state. Thermal switches, devices whose conductance changes with
temperature or another stimulus, are reviewed in general in~\cite{wehmeyer2017switches}
and for batteries in~\cite{li2025switches}, and a temperature-responsive regulator for
battery modules has been demonstrated experimentally~\cite{wang2024regulator}.

\paragraph{This work.}
This report complements that work with a deliberately minimal model that isolates the
conductivity-switching function of a barrier, and maps which switch properties are
needed for containment without penalising normal operation. It asks five questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does an idealised switchable barrier deliver both the conductor's normal-use
        temperature and the insulator's containment?
  \item What hot-state conductivity and thickness does it need?
  \item How should its switch temperature be chosen?
  \item Which of six real barrier materials, with properties taken from datasheets and
        the literature, best balances containment against normal-use temperature?
  \item Which filled-silicone formulation (fillers, nanoparticles, nanowires and their
        combinations) should be made and tested, and how robust is that choice?
\end{enumerate}
The emphasis is on a transparent, verified model rather than quantitative prediction.
The cell and kinetic parameters are illustrative, order-of-magnitude values and have
not been fitted to any particular cell. The barrier properties in the material comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:materials}) are sourced, with every assumed value flagged.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Geometry and governing equations}

The stack is five cells of thickness $L_c = 10$\,mm separated by four barriers of
thickness $L$ (default 2\,mm), modelled in one dimension through the stack thickness.
Temperature obeys transient conduction with a temperature-dependent conductivity and
volumetric sources,
\begin{equation}
  \rho c_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
  = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(k(T)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right)
  + q_\mathrm{rxn} + q_\mathrm{gen},
  \label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
with convective boundary conditions at both ends of the stack,
\begin{equation}
  -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x=0} = h\,(T_\infty - T), \qquad
  k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x=L_\mathrm{stack}} = h\,(T_\infty - T).
  \label{eq:bc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cell self-heating}

Each cell node carries a lumped reaction progress $\alpha \in [0,1]$ governed by
single-step kinetics, a simplification of the multi-reaction schemes used in detailed
abuse models~\cite{hatchard2001,spotnitz2003,kim2007abuse}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dd\alpha}{\dd t} = k_\mathrm{eff}(T)\,(1-\alpha), \qquad
  q_\mathrm{rxn} = \Delta H_v \frac{\dd\alpha}{\dd t}, \qquad
  \Delta H_v = \rho c_p\,\Delta T_\mathrm{ad},
  \label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
so that a cell reacting adiabatically heats by exactly $\Delta T_\mathrm{ad}$. The rate
constant combines an Arrhenius term with a smooth ceiling,
\begin{equation}
  k_\mathrm{eff} = \frac{k_\mathrm{arr}\,\kmax}{k_\mathrm{arr} + \kmax}, \qquad
  k_\mathrm{arr} = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}.
  \label{eq:keff}
\end{equation}
With $E_a = 169$\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ and $A = 7.3\times10^{16}$\,s$^{-1}$, $k_\mathrm{arr}$
is about $10^{-4}$\,s$^{-1}$ at 150\,$^\circ$C (slow onset) and about 1\,s$^{-1}$ at
250\,$^\circ$C. The ceiling $\kmax = 1$\,s$^{-1}$ leaves onset unchanged; its purpose is
explained in Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}.

\subsection{Barrier designs}

Four designs are compared: no barrier (cells in direct contact); a static conductor
($k = 5$\,W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$); a static, aerogel-like insulator
($k = 0.03$\,W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$); and a switchable barrier that conducts below a
switch temperature $\Tsw$ and insulates above it. The switch law is a logistic blend in
$\ln k$,
\begin{equation}
  \ln k(T) = \ln \koff + \frac{\ln(\kon/\koff)}{1 + e^{(T-\Tsw)/w}},
  \label{eq:switch}
\end{equation}
with $\kon = 5$\,W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$, $\koff = 0.05$\,W\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$,
$\Tsw = 120\,^\circ$C and $w = 5$\,K. The switch is reversible and local: every barrier
node switches on its own temperature, so a barrier with a steep internal gradient can be
insulating on its hot side and conducting on its cold side. Blending $\ln k$ rather than
$k$ matters for a 100$\times$ switch ratio. A linear blend leaves $k$ several times
$\koff$ until about 20\,K above $\Tsw$, so the barrier would barely insulate near its
nominal switch point. All barriers share the same volumetric heat capacity, which
isolates the effect of conductivity.

\subsection{Real barrier materials}
\label{sec:materials-model}

Comparing real materials needs three extensions of the barrier model. Each reduces to the
idealised designs when switched off, and all earlier results are unchanged.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tabulated conductivity.} $k(T)$ is interpolated from published points,
        linearly in $\ln k$, and held constant beyond the first and last point.
  \item \textbf{Latent heat.} A phase-change barrier stores enthalpy
        \begin{equation}
          H(T) = \rho c_p (T - T_\mathrm{ref}) + \rho L\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{T - T_m}{\sigma_m}\right),
          \label{eq:enthalpy}
        \end{equation}
        with latent heat $L$ released over a Gaussian melting range ($\Phi$ is the normal
        cumulative distribution, $\sigma_m = 3$\,K).
  \item \textbf{Switch dynamics.} The conducting fraction $s$ of a switch (the exponent
        in Eq.~\eqref{eq:switch}) relaxes toward its equilibrium value $s_\mathrm{eq}(T)$
        with time constant $\tau$, $\dd s/\dd t = (s_\mathrm{eq} - s)/\tau$. A one-way
        switch can only move toward the insulating state, $s^{n+1} = \min(s^n, \cdot)$.
\end{itemize}
Six candidates were chosen to cover the main barrier mechanisms
(Table~\ref{tab:materials}): three static insulators (silica aerogel blanket, phlogopite
mica paper, alumina--silica ceramic fibre), a static conductor (silicone gap pad), a
phase-change composite (paraffin with 10\,wt\% expanded graphite) and a thermal switch
(the graphene/expandable-microsphere regulator of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024regulator}).
Every value is taken from a manufacturer datasheet or a peer-reviewed paper, except
where marked as assumed. The data and their provenance are kept in
\texttt{runaway/candidates.py}, which also records how each material is mapped onto the
model.

\subsection{Filled-silicone compositions}
\label{sec:composites-model}

Many practical barriers and gap fillers are polymers loaded with particles. To compare
compositions rather than finished products, a silicone elastomer
matrix~\cite{sylgard184} ($k_m = 0.20$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $\rho = 1030$\,kg\,m$^{-3}$)
is combined with eight fillers (Table~\ref{tab:fillers}): calcium carbonate as
nanoparticles and microparticles, the flame-retardant hydroxides Al(OH)$_3$ (ATH) and
Mg(OH)$_2$ (MDH), hexagonal boron nitride platelets, silver nanowires, multi-walled carbon
nanotubes, and hollow glass microspheres.

\paragraph{Effective conductivity.} The through-plane conductivity of the composite is
estimated with the effective-medium model of Nan et al.~\cite{nan1997} for randomly
oriented spheroidal inclusions with an interfacial (Kapitza) resistance $R_K$. For filler
volume fractions $f_i$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{k}{k_m} = \frac{3 + \sum_i f_i\,[2\beta_{11}(1-L_{11}) + \beta_{33}(1-L_{33})]}
                       {3 - \sum_i f_i\,[2\beta_{11}L_{11} + \beta_{33}L_{33}]},\qquad
  \beta_{jj} = \frac{k^c_{jj} - k_m}{k_m + L_{jj}(k^c_{jj} - k_m)},
  \label{eq:nan}
\end{equation}
where $L_{jj}$ are the depolarisation factors of a spheroid with aspect ratio
$p = a_3/a_1$, and $k^c_{jj} = k_{p,jj}/(1 + \gamma L_{jj} k_{p,jj}/k_m)$ is the
particle conductivity reduced by the interface, with $\gamma = (2 + 1/p)\,a_K/a_1$ for
rods ($p \ge 1$), $\gamma = (1 + 2p)\,a_K/a_3$ for platelets ($p \le 1$) and Kapitza
radius $a_K = R_K k_m$. For spheres Eq.~\eqref{eq:nan} reduces to the Hasselman--Johnson
result and, with $R_K = 0$, to Maxwell's. Particle size enters only through $a_K/a$:
when the Kapitza radius approaches the particle size, the interface dominates, which is
why nanoparticles conduct less than microparticles of the same material. The model
neglects percolating filler networks, so it is used only below each shape's
percolation range (40\,vol\% for spheres, 30\,\% for platelets, 5\,\% for wires and
tubes). For the very high aspect ratios of nanowires and nanotubes it is an upper bound:
contact resistance, waviness and agglomeration make measured composites conduct less.

\paragraph{Endothermic fillers.} ATH, MDH and CaCO$_3$ decompose on heating, releasing
water or CO$_2$ and absorbing heat. Each is represented with the latent-heat mechanism of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:enthalpy}, carrying the filler's mass fraction of its decomposition
enthalpy over a Gaussian temperature range. The gas release, and the irreversibility of
the reaction, are not modelled. Density and heat capacity follow volume- and
mass-weighted mixture rules.

\subsection{Formulation design: nanowires, hybrids and an expanding switch}
\label{sec:formulation-model}

The eleven fixed compositions sample single fillers. Designing a formulation needs the
model to answer three further questions: how much do nanowires really add, what happens
when nanoparticles and nanowires are combined, and can a composite be made to switch?
Four extensions, implemented in \texttt{runaway/formulation.py}, address these. Each
reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:nan} when its ingredient is absent.

\paragraph{Fin-limited nanowires.} Applied to a nanowire, Eq.~\eqref{eq:nan} lets the
wire carry heat along its whole length. For aspect ratios of 100--1000 this predicts
about 5\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ for 5\,vol\% silver nanowires in silicone
(Fig.~\ref{fig:compk}), several times what is measured in comparable composites. Heat
has to enter and leave a wire through its surface, across the interface resistance and
the polymer around it. The wire therefore behaves as a fin with exchange coefficient
$h_\mathrm{eff} = [R_K + D/(2k_h)]^{-1}$, where $D$ is the wire diameter and $k_h$ the
conductivity of the host it sits in. Its effective axial conductivity is
\begin{equation}
  k_\mathrm{ax} = \eta\,k_w, \qquad \eta = \frac{\tanh(m\ell)}{m\ell}, \qquad
  m = \sqrt{\frac{4h_\mathrm{eff}}{k_w D}},
  \label{eq:fin}
\end{equation}
with $\ell$ half the wire length. $k_\mathrm{ax}$ replaces $k_{p,33}$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nan}. For a 40\,nm\,$\times$\,40\,$\mu$m wire in silicone,
$\eta \approx 0.03$. The intrinsic wire conductivity falls with diameter because of
boundary scattering. It is taken as $k_w = 429/(1 + D_0/D)$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ with
$D_0 = 24.5$\,nm, which reproduces the measured 332\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ at
84\,nm~\cite{agnw_nsf} and bulk silver for thick wires (an assumed form).

\paragraph{Percolating wire networks.} Randomly oriented slender rods first form a
connected network when the number density times their mean excluded volume reaches
one~\cite{balberg1984,florian2001}. For rods of length $L$ and diameter $D$ this gives the
threshold $\phi_c = D/(2L)$, about 0.05\,vol\% for 40\,nm\,$\times$\,40\,$\mu$m wires.
Above it, heat can also cross wire--wire junctions. Treating each wire as isothermal and
summing the heat dissipated over the junctions in a uniform gradient gives
$k_\mathrm{net} = (\pi/72)\,n^2 L^4 D\,G_c$ for $n$ wires per unit volume and junction
conductance $G_c$, the $n^2$ scaling found for nanotube
networks~\cite{volkov2010}. With $G_c = g\,k_h D$ (a junction bridged by polymer, $g$
a dimensionless junction coefficient) and the 3D conductivity exponent of two near the
threshold,
\begin{equation}
  k_\mathrm{net} = \frac{2}{9\pi}\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)^{2} g\,k_h\,(\phi_w - \phi_c)^2 ,
  \label{eq:net}
\end{equation}
which is added in parallel to the dispersed-wire result. Polymer composites show little
or no thermal percolation in practice~\cite{shenogina2005}, so $g$ is small by default
($g = 0.05$) and is varied from 0 to 0.5, with a further $\times10$ for sintered junctions.

\paragraph{Nanoparticle--nanowire hybrids.} Particles and wires are combined by
homogenising in two steps. Spherical or platelet particles are first mixed into the
matrix with Eq.~\eqref{eq:nan} (fractions taken relative to the non-wire volume),
giving a host conductivity $k_h$. The wires are then embedded in that host. Particles help
wires in two ways: a better host raises $h_\mathrm{eff}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fin}, and it
raises the junction conductance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net}.

\paragraph{Expandable-microsphere switch.} Thermoplastic microspheres filled with a
volatile hydrocarbon expand irreversibly when heated through a supplier-specified
range. For the grade used here (Expancel 051 DU 40) expansion begins at 105--110\,$^\circ$C
and peaks at 142--151\,$^\circ$C~\cite{expancel051}. A volume fraction $\phi_s$ of spheres
expanding by a volume ratio $E$ dilutes every other phase by
$X = 1 + \phi_s(E-1)$ and turns the spheres into gas cells occupying $\phi_s E/X$
($k = 0.04$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, assumed). The hot-state conductivity $\koff$ is the
two-step model evaluated with the diluted fractions. If the dilution takes the wire
fraction below $\phi_c$ the network term vanishes, so wires can amplify the switch.
The opposite, conservative assumption keeps the network intact in the polymer struts
between the gas cells (``segregated''). Free expansion exceeds 40-fold (the datasheet's
expanded density is at most 25\,kg\,m$^{-3}$), but a cured elastomer resists it, so
$E = 5$ is assumed and varied from 2 to 20. The composite enters the solver as a
one-way switch of the kind in Section~\ref{sec:materials-model}: $\kon$ and $\koff$
as computed, $\Tsw = 127\,^\circ$C at the middle of the expansion range, a width such that
10--90\,\% of the change spans $T_\mathrm{start}$ to $T_\mathrm{max}$ ($w = 8.9$\,K), and
$\tau = 10$\,s as for the graphene switch. Barrier thickness and heat capacity are held at
their unexpanded values. A barrier that also grew thicker would insulate better, so this
is conservative for containment. ATH or MDH added to the formulation carries its
decomposition enthalpy as in Section~\ref{sec:composites-model}. Alumina particles
($k = 30$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$~\cite{kyocera_al2o3}) are added to the filler set as

Fig.~\ref{fig:compk}.

\subsection{Two-dimensional model with a cold plate}
\label{sec:model2d}

In the 1D model the only heat sink is the pair of stack ends, which exaggerates how much
an insulating barrier heats the pack in normal use. Real modules stand on a liquid-cooled
plate. The 2D model adds the cell height $y$ ($H = 100$\,mm):
\begin{equation}
  \rho c_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} =
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(k_x \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right) +
  \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(k_y \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right) + q_\text{rxn} + q_\text{gen},
  \label{eq:heat2d}
\end{equation}
with the 1D end condition at $x = 0, L$; a cold plate at $y = 0$
($h_\text{plate} = 200$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$ including the interface material, coolant at
25\,$^\circ$C) under both cells and barriers; and natural convection
($h = 5$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$) at the top. Cells are anisotropic: $k_x = 0.8$ is the
through-plane value of the 1D model and $k_y = 20$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ the in-plane
value along the electrode sheets. Barriers are isotropic. These values, like the cell
parameters, are illustrative. The trigger condition, kinetics and propagation criterion
are those of the 1D model, with the cell mean taken over its area.

\subsection{Scenarios and parameters}

\paragraph{Abuse.} Cell~1 starts at 300\,$^\circ$C and everything else at 25\,$^\circ$C.
A cell is counted as \emph{propagated} when its volume-mean $\alpha$ exceeds 0.9.

\paragraph{Normal operation.} Every cell generates a uniform
$q_\mathrm{gen} = 2\times10^4$\,W\,m$^{-3}$, the reaction is switched off (its rate is
negligible below 70\,$^\circ$C), and Eq.~\eqref{eq:heat} is solved to steady state. The
quantity of interest is the peak cell temperature.

from the initial estimates during development. First, the end cooling coefficient was
raised to $h = 100$\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$, representing an end plate connected to a
cooling system. With natural-convection values ($h \lesssim 30$\,W\,m$^{-2}$\,K$^{-1}$)
no barrier, not even the insulator, prevented propagation in this 1D model, because the
stack ends are the only heat sink and the trigger cell's roughly 20\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ must
eventually leave somewhere. Second, the rate ceiling $\kmax$ was added

\section{Numerical method}

\paragraph{Spatial discretisation.} Each layer is meshed separately (40 volumes per cell,
12 per barrier), so every material interface lies on a control-volume face. The face
conductance is the series resistance of the two adjoining half-volumes,
\begin{equation}
  G_{i+1/2} = \left( \frac{\Delta x_i}{2k_i} + \frac{\Delta x_{i+1}}{2k_{i+1}} \right)^{-1},
  \label{eq:face}
\end{equation}
which is the distance-weighted harmonic mean of $k$~\cite{patankar1980}. It is exact for
steady conduction through layers in series and handles the 27$\times$ jump at an
insulator--cell interface without special treatment. Boundary faces add $1/h$ in series.

\paragraph{Time integration.} Reaction and conduction are coupled by first-order (Lie)
operator splitting~\cite{strang1968}. In the \emph{reaction step}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kin} is
linear in $\alpha$ for a frozen rate constant and has the exact solution
\begin{equation}
  \alpha^{n+1} = 1 - (1-\alpha^n)\,e^{-k_\mathrm{eff}\Delta t},
  \label{eq:expupdate}
\end{equation}
where $k_\mathrm{eff}$ is evaluated at a predicted mid-step temperature (an exponential
midpoint rule). The released heat is added as
$\Delta T_\mathrm{ad}\,(\alpha^{n+1}-\alpha^n)$. The update is stable for any
$\Delta t$, keeps $0\le\alpha\le1$ and conserves energy exactly, which makes the stiff
kinetics tractable. In the \emph{conduction step}, backward Euler is used with $k(T)$
lagged from the start of the step (Picard refinement is available). The resulting
tridiagonal system is solved with a banded direct solver.

\paragraph{Step control.} The time step is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t = \min\!\left(\Delta t_\mathrm{max},\;
  \frac{\Delta T_\mathrm{rxn,max}}{\max_i \dot T_{\mathrm{rxn},i}}\right),
  \label{eq:dt}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta T_\mathrm{rxn,max} = 2$\,K and $\Delta t_\mathrm{max} = 0.5$\,s. This is an
accuracy limit rather than a stability limit: it shrinks the step only while some node
is igniting.

\paragraph{Steady state.} Normal operation is solved by pseudo-transient continuation:
backward-Euler steps from ambient with a geometrically growing step. For the switchable
barrier this follows the physical start-up path, so if self-heating trips the switch,
the solution lands on the hot (insulating) branch.

\paragraph{Latent heat.} With a phase-change barrier, the heat capacity in the
backward-Euler step is replaced by the chord capacity
$C^\ast = [H(T^{n+1}) - H(T^n)]/(T^{n+1} - T^n)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:enthalpy}, and the
step is iterated to a tolerance of $10^{-7}$\,K. The stored-energy change then equals the
enthalpy change exactly, so a node that crosses the whole melting range in one step
cannot skip the latent heat, which an apparent-heat-capacity method evaluated at the old
temperature would do.

\paragraph{Two dimensions.} The 2D solver uses the same scheme on a tensor-product mesh
(10 volumes per cell and 4 per barrier in $x$, 20 over the height), with harmonic-mean
face conductances in both directions and a sparse direct solve per step. The coarser $x$
mesh is checked in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

\paragraph{Energy accounting.} The solver tracks stored enthalpy, unreleased chemical
energy and cumulative boundary loss. Because both sub-steps are conservative, the
discrete energy balance closes to round-off. The largest relative residual in any run
reported here is $3\times10^{-10}$ (a phase-change barrier, set by the iteration
tolerance); without latent heat it is below $10^{-11}$.

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

The test suite runs automatically on every commit and checks the following.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Energy conservation.} With insulated ends and no reaction, total thermal
        energy stays constant to a relative $10^{-12}$ over 2000\,s, with the nonlinear
        switchable barrier in place. With the reaction on, thermal plus chemical energy
        is conserved, and with convection the budget closes to below $10^{-10}$.
  \item \textbf{Series conduction.} For a cell\,|\,barrier\,|\,cell stack held between
        two different ambient temperatures, the steady solution matches the analytical
        series-resistance profile to $10^{-8}$\,K, for both 6$\times$ and 27$\times$
        conductivity jumps, on a deliberately coarse mesh. For uniform generation in a
        single cell, the peak matches $T_\infty + qL_c/(2h) + qL_c^2/(8k)$.
  \item \textbf{Reaction.} A single adiabatic reacting node ends at exactly
        $T_0 + \Delta T_\mathrm{ad}$. Its time to 50\,\% conversion matches a
        tight-tolerance implicit ODE solution (Radau IIA~\cite{hairer1996}) to within
        1\,\%, with and without the rate ceiling.
  \item \textbf{Convergence.} The cell-2 propagation time converges under mesh
        refinement with an observed order above 1.5, and under refinement of
        $\Delta T_\mathrm{rxn,max}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:conv}).
  \item \textbf{Material extensions.} Tabulated $k(T)$ reproduces the published points.
        In an insulated stack with a phase-change barrier, enthalpy is conserved to
        $10^{-9}$ and the final uniform temperature satisfies the enthalpy balance
        including melting. A one-way switch heated past $\Tsw$ stays insulating after
        cooling, while a reversible one recovers, and a larger $\tau$ delays switching.
  \item \textbf{Composites.} For spheres the effective-medium model reproduces the
        Maxwell and Hasselman--Johnson formulas to $10^{-12}$; depolarisation factors
        satisfy $2L_{11} + L_{33} = 1$ and the rod and disc limits; and mixture rules
        conserve mass and latent heat.
  \item \textbf{Formulations.} Without wires or microspheres the two-step model equals
        the Nan model to $10^{-12}$; the dilute limit matches Maxwell's first-order
        result; the percolation threshold and the quadratic growth above it follow
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:net}; a short wire is nearly isothermal ($\eta > 0.95$); the two
        calibration points are reproduced to within 10\,\%; and expansion lowers $k$,
        more so for larger $E$, with the segregated network never insulating better
        than the dilute one.
  \item \textbf{2D solver.} With the cold plate and top removed, the 2D solver reproduces
        the 1D propagation times and normal-operation peak to $10^{-9}$. For a single
        uniformly heated cell cooled only by the plate it matches the analytical
        parabolic profile up to the expected $q\Delta y^2/8k_y$ boundary term, and the
        energy budget with plate cooling and latent heat closes to below $10^{-9}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why a rate ceiling is needed}
\label{sec:ceiling}

The kinetic parameters describe the onset region (150--250\,$^\circ$C). Extrapolated to
a burning cell near 900\,$^\circ$C, pure Arrhenius kinetics give
$k_\mathrm{arr}\sim10^9$\,s$^{-1}$, which implies a reaction front inside the cell of
order $\sqrt{\kappa/k_\mathrm{arr}}\sim 10^{-7}$\,m, where $\kappa = k/\rho c_p$ is the
thermal diffusivity. No practical mesh resolves this, so the front speed is set by the
mesh rather than by the physics. The convergence test exposed the problem. Without the
ceiling, the cell-2 propagation time drifts from 14.3 to 10.9, 11.1 and 8.8\,s as the
mesh is refined from 10 to 80 volumes per cell, with no sign of settling
(Fig.~\ref{fig:conv}).

Real high-temperature decomposition is limited by transport and multi-step chemistry,
not by an ever-faster single step. The smooth ceiling in Eq.~\eqref{eq:keff} with
$\kmax = 1$\,s$^{-1}$ makes a single cell burn over a few seconds, broadens the front to
about $\sqrt{\kappa/\kmax}\approx0.6$\,mm and restores convergence. The default mesh is
within 2\,\% of a 4$\times$ finer one, and halving $\Delta T_\mathrm{rxn,max}$ changes
the result by 0.1\,\%. The contained cases are also converged: the peak temperature in
cell~2 behind the switchable barrier varies by less than 0.1\,K over a 100$\times$ range
of $\Delta t_\mathrm{max}$, and by about 1\,K from 20 to 80 volumes per cell.

  stack. With pure Arrhenius kinetics the result does not converge, because the reaction

\subsection{Mechanism of containment}

Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows how the switch works. The hot face of the first barrier
switches off and holds a temperature drop of roughly 700\,K across a few millimetres
while cell~1 sheds its heat through the cooled end. The cold side of the same barrier
stays below $\Tsw$ and keeps conducting, as do the downstream barriers, so cell~2
spreads whatever heat leaks through into cells 3--5. One consequence is easy to miss.
Cell~2 reaches a higher local peak behind the switchable barrier (153\,$^\circ$C,
3\,\% conversion) than behind the static insulator (124\,$^\circ$C, 0.2\,\%), because
only part of the switchable barrier's thickness is in the insulating state.

\subsection{Design map}
\label{sec:results-map}

Figure~\ref{fig:map} varies the switchable barrier's thickness $L$ (0.5--5\,mm) and
hot-state conductivity $\koff$ (0.02--1\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) over 361 simulations. Two
features stand out. First, the outcomes are binary: every run ends with either one or
all five cells in runaway. Once cell~2 ignites, each later cell faces a trigger at least
as severe, so the barrier must stop the first hand-off. Second, the boundary follows a
line of constant hot-state conductance, $\koff/L \approx 32$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$, about
a third of the end-cooling coefficient. Physically, the runaway cell must lose heat to
the cooled end faster than it leaks into its neighbour. The default design
($\koff/L = 25$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$) lies about 20\,\% inside the boundary, a modest
margin.

\subsection{Choosing the switch temperature}

\paragraph{No candidate contains runaway at 2\,mm.} Even the aerogel, whose room-temperature
$k$ is below that of the idealised insulator, lets runaway through at 2\,mm. Its
conductivity triples between 200 and 600\,$^\circ$C, the range the barrier's hot face
reaches, so its effective hot-state conductance at 2\,mm is about 40\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$,
above the containment threshold of Section~\ref{sec:results-map}. What matters for
containment is a barrier's conductivity at runaway temperatures, which datasheets often
report only incompletely.

\paragraph{Static insulators trade thickness against normal-use temperature.} Aerogel
contains runaway with the thinnest layer, 2.25\,mm, but raises the normal-operation peak by
41\,K over the conductor. Ceramic fibre needs 4.25\,mm and costs 28\,K. Mica paper conducts
heat about ten times better than aerogel, so its normal-use penalty is small (11\,K), but
it needs 6.25\,mm before its conductance falls below the threshold. For all three the
penalty grows steadily with thickness (Fig.~\ref{fig:materials}a).

\paragraph{The conductor and the phase-change composite fail.} The gap pad behaves like the
idealised conductor and never contains runaway. The PCM also conducts well
($k \approx 10$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$), so it keeps cells cool, and its latent heat does
slow propagation: at 4\,mm, runaway reaches cell~5 after 148\,s instead of 100\,s without
latent heat. But a 4\,mm layer stores only about 1\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ of latent heat, against
about 15\,MJ\,m$^{-2}$ of reaction heat released by each cell, and once melted it passes
heat on readily. Only at 9.5--10\,mm does it hold runaway to two cells.

\paragraph{The thermal switch avoids the trade-off.} The graphene switch contains runaway at
3.5\,mm while keeping the normal-operation peak at 38.9\,$^\circ$C, within 1\,K of the ideal
conductor. Its hot-state conductance at that thickness,
$0.10/0.0035 \approx 29$\,W\,m$^{-2}$K$^{-1}$, is just below the threshold found with the
idealised switch, so the design-map criterion carries over to a real material. Because
its cold-state conductivity is only 1.33\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, the normal-use penalty does
grow with thickness, but slowly (41\,$^\circ$C at 10\,mm).

\paragraph{Sensitivity.} The switch data come from a paper abstract, so its density, heat
capacity, switch temperature and response time are uncertain. Table~\ref{tab:sens} varies
each one. Across these cases the thinnest containing switch ranges from 3.25 to 5\,mm,
with the normal-operation peak between 38.8 and 39.3\,$^\circ$C. The result is most
sensitive to the hot-state conductivity, as the $\koff/L$ criterion predicts, and to a
slow response: with $\tau = 30$\,s the switch needs 4.5\,mm. Reversibility makes no
difference here, because the barrier next to the runaway cell does not cool below $\Tsw$
during the run. Doubling the switch's assumed heat capacity, or cutting the aerogel's by
30\,\%, changes its thinnest containing barrier by at most 0.25\,mm.

\paragraph{Which material is best?} Judged on both criteria together, the graphene switch is
the best of the six. It is the only candidate that contains runaway at a practical
thickness without a normal-use penalty, and it stays the best in every sensitivity case.
Aerogel is the choice if thickness is the only constraint and the pack can remove the
extra heat another way, for example with side cooling, which this 1D model cannot
represent. Among commercially established products, mica paper is the best compromise if
about 6\,mm per gap and a normal-use peak near 49\,$^\circ$C are acceptable. The switch
result rests on a laboratory material characterised here only by its published headline
numbers, so measured $k(T)$, heat capacity and cycling data are needed before it can be
recommended for a real pack.

\subsection{Formulation design}
\label{sec:formulation}

The products above are fixed. This section uses the model of
Section~\ref{sec:formulation-model} to ask what a silicone barrier should be made of.
Each formulation is scored like the products: the thinnest barrier that confines runaway
to the trigger cell, found by bisection to 0.125\,mm between 0.25 and 10\,mm, and the
normal-operation peak at that thickness. The reference materials were rescored the same
way. On the finer grid mica contains at 6.125\,mm (48.5\,$^\circ$C) and ceramic fibre at
4.125\,mm (65.3\,$^\circ$C); the other values match Table~\ref{tab:matresults}.

\paragraph{Screen.} The screen crossed 21 conductive systems with five microsphere
fractions (0--40\,vol\%) and five endothermic options (none, or 10 or 20\,vol\% ATH or
MDH), keeping total filler at or below 60\,vol\% as a castability limit (assumed). That
gave 452 formulations. The conductive systems were: none; silica-coated silver nanowires of three
sizes (40\,nm\,$\times$\,40\,$\mu$m, 60\,nm\,$\times$\,20\,$\mu$m,
100\,nm\,$\times$\,15\,$\mu$m) at 0.5--3\,vol\%; h-BN at 10 and 20\,vol\%;
5\,$\mu$m alumina at 20 and 30\,vol\%; and four hybrids of 1 or 2\,vol\% of the thinnest
wires with 10\,vol\% h-BN or 50\,nm alumina. Of the 452, 361 contain runaway at some
thickness up to 10\,mm (Fig.~\ref{fig:formscreen}).

\paragraph{Static formulations repeat the material trade-off.} Without microspheres a
formulation is a static barrier, and the $\koff/L$ criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:results-map} applies to its single conductivity. Only 55 of the 105 static
formulations contain runaway at all. Neat silicone needs 5.6\,mm and peaks at 49.1\,$^\circ$C,
close to mica. Filling raises $k$, which lowers the normal-use peak but raises the
thickness needed: the best static formulation (30\,vol\% alumina + 20\,vol\% ATH) peaks at
43.1\,$^\circ$C but needs 9.25\,mm. Static nanowire formulations contain only at low
loadings (0.5--2\,vol\%, $k \le 0.54$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) and then need 6--9.25\,mm.
Above about 0.5\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, which includes every h-BN formulation, they conduct
well enough that, like the gap pad, they never contain.
No choice of particle, nanoparticle or nanowire escapes the conflict, because a static
filler cannot tell normal operation from runaway.

\paragraph{Microspheres are the main lever.} Adding expandable microspheres turns the
composite into a one-way switch, and 306 of the 347 switching formulations contain.
Figure~\ref{fig:formscreen}b shows that the microsphere fraction matters more than the
choice of conductive filler. Every formulation on the screen's thickness--temperature
Pareto front used the largest fraction screened, 40\,vol\%:

A second pass extended the fraction to 45 and 50\,vol\%. 50\,vol\% microspheres with
10\,vol\% ATH contains at 2.375\,mm (42.5\,$^\circ$C), a small further gain at a filler
loading that is likely to be hard to process. Figure~\ref{fig:formswitch}a,b shows why
the fraction dominates. Expansion lowers $\koff$ roughly in proportion to the gas-cell
fraction it creates, while the cold-state $\kon$ barely changes. The expansion ratio $E$
matters as much: at 40\,vol\% the thinnest containing barrier goes from 4.1\,mm at
$E = 2$ to 1.9\,mm at $E = 20$. Even at $E = 2$ (4.1\,mm, 44.0\,$^\circ$C) it needs less than half
the thickness of the best static formulation at a similar peak (9.25\,mm,
43.1\,$^\circ$C).

\paragraph{Nanowires help only if expansion breaks their network.} Nanowires are the most
efficient way to raise $\kon$, and the best formulations in the screen contained them:
3\,vol\% wires with 30\,vol\% microspheres and 20\,vol\% ATH peaks at 39.5\,$^\circ$C,
contained at 5.1\,mm. Their weakness is the hot state. The wires that raise $\kon$ also
raise $\koff$, unless expansion pushes them below the percolation threshold. With
40\,nm\,$\times$\,40\,$\mu$m wires the threshold is only 0.05\,vol\%, so a five-fold
expansion of 40\,vol\% microspheres ($X = 2.6$) leaves 1--3\,vol\% loadings far above it.
Figure~\ref{fig:formswitch}c shows the consequence. With weak junctions ($g \le 0.05$)
and a dilute network, wires cost a little thickness and buy a little temperature. With
strong junctions ($g = 0.5$) the thinnest containing barrier grows to 5.9\,mm at
2\,vol\% and 8.4\,mm at 3\,vol\%. If the network also survives in the polymer struts,
1\,vol\% of strongly joined wires, or 3\,vol\% of weakly joined ones, never contains. The formulation model
cannot say which case real composites fall in. Measured nanowire composites show weak
junctions (Section~\ref{sec:formulation-model}), but these were dispersed in a rigid
epoxy and never expanded. Hybrids with nanoparticles did not improve on wires alone in
the screen. Nanoparticles raise $\kon$ slightly through the host conductivity, but they
also stay in the hot state and raise $\koff$, and the model credits them with little of
the particle-bridging synergy seen in experiments~\cite{hu2021agnwal2o3}.

\paragraph{Endothermic fillers.} ATH and MDH absorb 0.4--0.5\,MJ\,kg$^{-1}$ of the
composite at 20\,vol\%, but in this model that does little for containment. At 40\,vol\%
microspheres, adding 20\,vol\% ATH lengthens the thinnest containing barrier from 2.375
to 2.875\,mm, because the particles ($k_p = 3$\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$) raise $\koff$. It also
lowers the normal-use peak by 1.5\,K, because they raise $\kon$. ATH matches or beats MDH in
all 139 pairings that contain (2.875 vs.\ 3.0\,mm at 20\,vol\%), since it decomposes earlier
(260 vs.\ 365\,$^\circ$C). The main reason to keep it is outside the model: ATH is a
flame retardant whose water release protects the silicone, which this model does not
represent.

\paragraph{Selection.} The final choice weighs worst cases, not baselines. The six
Pareto-front formulations were rerun under every input the data leave open: the
expansion ratio ($E = 3$, 10), a segregated network, the gas-cell conductivity
(0.06\,W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$), a higher-temperature microsphere grade (Expancel 920,
expanding at 122--206\,$^\circ$C~\cite{expancel920}), switch response
($\tau = 0$, 30\,s), heat capacity ($\times0.7$) and, for nanowires, junction strength
($g = 0$, 0.5, sintered), poor dispersion and halved wire conductivity. The
criterion is the lowest worst-case normal-use peak among formulations whose worst-case
containing thickness is at most 4\,mm, roughly that of the graphene switch:

6\,K cooler than mica at less than half mica's thickness. It does not match the graphene
switch, which contains at 3.5\,mm with a 38.9\,$^\circ$C peak. The graphene switch's
cold-state conductivity is five times higher, and a microsphere composite can match that
only with a conductive network that expansion reliably breaks. Whether nanowire or
graphene networks in an expanding silicone actually break is the key experiment this
study points to. If they do, the 2--3\,vol\% nanowire formulations reach
39.6--40.5\,$^\circ$C at 3.6--4.3\,mm. If they do not, the filler-free formulation
above is the safe choice.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

The model is deliberately minimal, and several simplifications bound what it can say.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One dimension.} Heat flows only through the stack thickness, and the
        stack ends are the only heat sink. Real packs also lose heat sideways to cold
        plates, housings and busbars. This is why the results depend strongly on $h$.
        The containment threshold $\koff/L \approx h/3$ should be read as a statement
        about the competition between barrier leakage and heat removal, not as a design
        rule.
  \item \textbf{Lumped kinetics.} One Arrhenius step with a rate ceiling captures onset
        and total heat release, but not the sequence of real decomposition reactions
        (SEI breakdown, anode--electrolyte, cathode decomposition, electrolyte
        combustion, internal short)~\cite{feng2018review,kim2007abuse}.
  \item \textbf{Missing transport.} Venting, ejecta, gas flow and flames, which often
        carry much of the energy in real failures~\cite{wang2012fire}, are not modelled,
        and neither are radiation across gaps or contact resistance.
  \item \textbf{Simplified switching.} The idealised switch is instantaneous and
        reversible. The real-material model adds a response time and one-way switching,
        but not hysteresis, degradation over cycles or latent heat of the switch itself.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete material data.} Several heat capacities are assumed, the gap
        pad and PCM are extrapolated far beyond their rated temperatures, the aerogel
        and ceramic fibre data are for industrial blankets rather than battery sheets,
        and the switch is described by two published points. Decomposition, melting
        leakage and combustion of organic barriers are not modelled.
  \item \textbf{Formulation model.} The fin and network models are mean-field estimates,
        checked against two measured nanowire composites in epoxy, not silicone. The
        junction coefficient, the constrained expansion ratio of microspheres in a cured
        silicone and whether expansion breaks a wire network are not known and are
        varied instead. The model under-predicts nanoparticle--nanowire synergy, ignores
        the viscosity limits of high loadings beyond a 60\,vol\% cap, and treats
        the expanded state as fixed: thermoplastic shells soften and collapse well
        below the barrier's hot-face temperature, and the silicone itself degrades
        above roughly 300\,$^\circ$C. The selected formulation is the one to make and
        measure ($k(T)$ through expansion, expansion ratio under the stack's
        compression), not a validated product.
  \item \textbf{Illustrative cell parameters.} The cell and kinetic values are not fitted
        to data, so temperatures and times are qualitative. The material ranking is more
        robust than the absolute thicknesses, which scale with the end cooling $h$.
\end{itemize}
Natural next steps are to measure $k(T)$ up to runaway temperatures for the leading
candidates, especially the thermal switch, including hysteresis and cycling; to fit
multi-step kinetics to accelerating-rate calorimetry and calibrate $\kmax$ against measured
runaway duration; to extend the model to 2D or 3D with lateral cooling, which would reduce
the normal-use penalty of insulators; to compare against an independent commercial
finite-element model; and to quantify how parameter uncertainty moves the containment
boundary.

\section{Conclusions}

A verified 1D finite-volume model reproduces the barrier conflict between normal-use
cooling and abuse containment, and shows that an idealised switchable barrier resolves
it. Containment is controlled by the hot-state barrier conductance relative to the heat
removal available to the runaway cell. The switch temperature has a broad working window
between the normal operating temperature and the onset of self-heating. On the numerical
side, mesh-convergence testing exposed a modelling error that a single run would hide:
onset-fitted Arrhenius kinetics extrapolated to burning temperatures make propagation
times mesh-dependent, and a physically motivated rate ceiling is needed for a converged
answer.

Applied to six real barrier materials, the model shows that the conflict is real for
static materials. Insulators that contain runaway either raise normal-use temperatures
by 28--41\,K (aerogel, ceramic fibre) or need a 6\,mm layer (mica), and a conductive pad
or phase-change composite does not contain runaway at all. A published graphene/microsphere
thermal switch is the best candidate: it contains runaway at about 3.5\,mm with a
normal-use temperature within 1\,K of an ideal conductor, a result that holds across all
sensitivity cases. Its standing depends on measured high-temperature properties that are
not yet available.

A formulation study then asked what a silicone barrier should be made of. With wires
treated as fins and wire networks as a percolating junction network, the model
reproduces measured silver-nanowire composites to within about 10\,\%. Per unit volume,
nanowires are the most effective way to raise cold-state conductivity, but no static
filler, particle, nanoparticle, nanowire or hybrid escapes the containment conflict.
Expandable microspheres turn the composite into a one-way switch, and their fraction
and expansion ratio dominate everything else. Nanowires help only if expansion breaks
their network, and they make containment much worse if it does not. The recommended
formulation, silicone with 40\,vol\% Expancel 051 microspheres and 20\,vol\% Al(OH)$_3$, contains
runaway at 2.9\,mm with a 42\,$^\circ$C normal-use peak and stays within 3.5\,mm and
43\,$^\circ$C across all sensitivity cases. That makes it the strongest option that can be
formulated from commercial ingredients, second only to the laboratory graphene switch.
The decisive next measurements are the constrained expansion ratio and whether a
nanowire network survives expansion.
